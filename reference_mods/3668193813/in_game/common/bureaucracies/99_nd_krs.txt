# 1. Hakim Sancaks -- the hereditary Kurdish governorships established by
#    Selim I on the advice of Idris-i Bidlisi after Chaldiran (1514). Unlike
#    standard Ottoman sancaks (rotated, bureaucratically appointed), the
#    Hakkari, Bitlis, Cizre/Bohtan, Imadiya/Bahdinan and other Kurdish emirates
#    were ocaklik -- hereditary in the local mir's house, exempt from
#    devshirme, retaining their tribal contingents and local courts. The
#    settlement gave the Sublime Porte a loyal eastern frontier; it gave the
#    Kurdish mirs a permanent constitutional shield around their autonomy.

# 2. Sharafnama Register -- the genealogical-political chronicle Prince
#    Sharaf Khan of Bitlis completed in Persian in 1597, cataloguing the
#    Marwanid, Hasanwayhid, Fadluyid, Ayyubid, and contemporary Kurdish
#    dynasties down to the emirates of his own day. Treated as a state
#    institution, the register fixed the genealogical claims of every great
#    house, settled questions of precedence at court, and gave the Kurdish
#    nation a documented past coextensive with its mountain geography. The
#    Sharafnama was copied, recopied, and continued by later chroniclers --
#    the closest thing the Kurdish polity had to a chancery archive.

# 3. Naqshbandi-Khalidi Tariqat -- the Sufi order reformed and propagated by
#    Mawlana Khalid al-Shahrazuri (d. 1827) of Sulaymaniyah, which by the
#    mid-nineteenth century had supplanted the older Qadiriya as the dominant
#    network of religious authority across Kurdistan. The Khalidi sheikhs
#    governed extensive lodges (tekiyes) and disciple chains; with the
#    progressive collapse of the great mirate houses, the agha-sheikh
#    combination took up local arbitration, tribal mediation, and even tax
#    collection. By the late Ottoman era the Naqshbandi sheikhs were the
#    real power-brokers of much of Kurdish society, often explicitly at the

# 4. Mirate Diwan -- the deliberative council of the great Kurdish emirates,
