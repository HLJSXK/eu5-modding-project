# National Destinies -- Kanem-Bornu bureaucracies
# Five historically grounded institutions of the Sayfawa Mais, spanning the
# Kanem phase (c. 1075-1380) through the Bornu phase (1380-1846) of the
# longest-reigning dynasty in the medieval and early-modern world. Sources
# rest on the Diwan salatin Bornu, the Girgam, the chronicle of Ahmad ibn
# Furtu (Kitab ghazawat Idris) on Mai Idris Alooma's reign 1571-1603, and
# the institutional studies of Yves Urvoy, Dierk Lange, and John Lavers on
# the Sayfawa state.
#
# Author-modifier grants 2 slots; capital adds 1 = 3 effective. Player picks
# 3 of 5. The five institutions cut across distinct power axes: the council
# of officers (crown + nobles), the queen-mother's parallel court (an
# institutional check on the Mai), the Ottoman-trained mamlukan musketeers
# (a gunpowder revolution dependent on slave soldiers), the Arabic chancery
# and royal chronicle (a literate scribal class), and the pastoral tribute
# compact (a working accommodation with the Kanembu-Toubou-Tuareg nomads).

# 1. Council of the Twelve -- the institutional core of Sayfawa governance.
#    The Mai ruled through twelve great offices (the maina), each with its
#    own hereditary or appointive succession: the Galadima as commander of
#    the western marches and chancellor; the Yerima as governor of the north
#    and warden of the salt routes; the Kaigamarama as commander of the
#    royal slave corps; the Mestrema as treasurer and master of horse; the
#    Cirima as guardian of the youngest princes; and the lesser offices
#    governing the wards of Birni Gazargamo and the provincial sanjaks.
#    Each office carried its own fief, retainers, and seat at the council;
#    the Mai's word required the council's assent to be binding in custom.

# 2. Magira's Court -- the institutionalised office of the queen-mother,
#    held since the early Kanem period as a constitutional check on the
#    Mai. The Magira commanded her own treasury, retinue, and palace at
#    Birni Gazargamo, and her formal consent was required for the
#    enthronement of a new Mai. The most famous holder, Aisa Kili
#    Ngirmaramma (regent c. 1564-70), governed Bornu in her own name
#    during the minority of Idris Alooma and brokered the Ottoman
#    embassy that brought Turkish musket-trainers to the kingdom. The
#    institution was Sayfawa-specific and unparalleled in the wider
#    Sahel; the ulama tolerated it as ancestral custom while privately

# 3. Mamlukan Musketeers -- the slave-soldier infantry corps founded by
#    Mai Idris Alooma after his diplomatic mission of 1574 to the
#    Ottoman governor of Tripoli, Mustafa Pasha. Turkish drillmasters
#    instructed Bornu's slave-soldiers in the use of the matchlock; the
#    corps was housed at the royal court, drew its salt and provisions
#    directly from the Mai's stores, and answered to the Kaigamarama
#    rather than to any provincial lord. Its recruits were drawn from
#    the southern slaving frontiers and from purchase. The corps broke
#    the Bulala at Garni Kiyala in 1577 and forced the surrender of the
#    Bilala kingdom; under its successors it remained the spine of

# 4. Sayfawa Girgam -- the Arabic-language chancery and royal chronicle
#    of the Sayfawa Mais, maintained without break from the eleventh
#    century to the fall of the dynasty. The Girgam (the proper name of
#    the chronicle) recorded each Mai's accession, campaigns, marriages,
#    and grants; it was supplemented by the Diwan salatin Bornu and by
#    the Kitab ghazawat Idris of Ahmad ibn Furtu, the Mai's imam-
#    chronicler. The chancery employed scribes drawn from the ulama and
#    the Wangara diaspora; their writs gave juridical force to royal
#    grants and bound succeeding generations to the obligations of their
#    fathers. The institution proved hard to dismantle: a century after
#    the Sayfawa fell, the Shehu's court still preserved their archive.

# 5. Kambasokis Compact -- the working tribute-and-grazing arrangement
#    by which the Sayfawa Mais bound the pastoral peoples of the basin
#    -- the Kanembu, the Toubou (Tubu), the Buduma fishermen of the
#    Lake Chad islands, and the Tuareg of the northern marches -- to
#    the throne. The compact obliged each people to render an annual
#    tribute (cattle, salt-bars, slaves, horses) and a seasonal levy
#    of cavalry; in exchange the Mai's troops kept the wells open, the
#    raiders out, and the grazing rotations enforced. The compact ran
#    in customary law rather than written code, and broke down only
#    when the Fulani jihad upset the Sahelian balance. Its working
#    fell heaviest on the sedentary cultivators along the Komadugu
#    Yobe, who shouldered the cost of nomad protection.
