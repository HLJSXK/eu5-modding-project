# National Destinies -- Kingdom of Benin (Edo / Bini) bureaucracies
# Six historical institutions of the Benin state (c.1180-1897, with Ewuare's
# reforms c.1440 producing the dual-hierarchy that defined the kingdom for
# four centuries):
#   - the Uzama Nihinron seven hereditary kingmaker-clans
#   - the Eghaevbo n'Ogbe palace-chief hierarchy organized through the three
#     palace societies (Iwebo, Iweguae, Ibiwe) created to counterbalance the Uzama
#   - the Igun-Eronmwon royal brass-casters' guild (monopoly on commemorative
#     plaques and ivory carving) housed within the Iwebo
#   - the Igue annual purification festival, Ewuare's calendar of divine
#     kingship that gathered every chief to renew the Oba's sovereignty
#   - the Iyase senior town-chief / war-commander office, a hereditary
#     military counterpoise to the throne whose holders periodically rebelled
#   - the Ologbo'na resident-governors over tributary Yoruba and Edo towns
#     (Akure, Owo, Ekiti, Mahin, the Lagos foundation)
# Auto-modifier grants 2 slots; Benin City contributes 1 = 3 effective. The
# player picks 3 of 6. The set models a polity whose centralization story is
# a four-century quarrel between hereditary kingmakers, a household-bureaucratic
# counter-aristocracy, and a famously rebellious war-commander -- a balance no
# other West African polity replicated in this exact shape.

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Uzama Nihinron -- the seven hereditary kingmaker chiefs of metropolitan Benin
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# The Uzama Nihinron ("the seven nobles") were the senior order of Edo
# nobility: Oliha, Edohen, Ezomo, Ero, Eholo n'Ire, Oloton, and Ihaza. Their
# offices were hereditary within seven specific clans of metropolitan Benin
# and predated the Oba dynasty itself -- tradition holds that it was the
# Uzama who in the late 12th century invited Oranmiyan of Ife to send a son
# (Eweka I) to rule them, founding the Oba dynasty around 1200. They retained
# the constitutional right to instal each new Oba: no king's accession was
# legitimate without the coronation ceremony at Usama, where the Oliha
# placed the beaded crown on his head. After Ewuare's reforms of c.1440
# they were stripped of administrative power but kept the kingmaker role,
# which made them a permanent counterweight to royal authority.

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Eghaevbo n'Ogbe -- the palace-chief hierarchy of the three royal societies
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Ewuare the Great (r. c.1440-1473) reorganized Benin governance around three
# palace societies, each headquartered in its own quarter of the Oba's
# residence: Iwebo (the chiefs of regalia, brass, and ivory), Iweguae (the
# chiefs of the king's person, household, and ceremonial), and Ibiwe (the
# chiefs of the harem and royal children). Each society admitted men by
# royal appointment rather than birth, and their titled members collectively
# formed the Eghaevbo n'Ogbe -- the "palace chiefs" -- a body deliberately
# constructed to give the Oba an administrative arm independent of the Uzama
# and the town-chiefs. Promotion within a palace society was personal,
# revocable, and tied directly to royal favor; the structure gave the throne
# the bureaucratic counter-aristocracy that Ewuare's centralization required.

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Igun-Eronmwon -- the royal guild of brass-casters and ivory-carvers
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# The Igun-Eronmwon, the Brass-Casters' Guild of Igun Street in Benin City,
# held the royal monopoly on the lost-wax casting of the commemorative
# plaques, heads, and figurines that adorned the Oba's palace -- the corpus
# now known to museums as the "Benin Bronzes" (in fact a leaded brass alloy
# imported via the trans-Saharan and later the Atlantic copper trade). The
# guild's master, the Inneh n'Igun-Eronmwon, ranked as a senior chief of
# the Iwebo palace society. A parallel guild, the Igbesanmwan of ivory
# carvers, supplied the carved tusks displayed on royal altars. These
# guilds were not merely artisan associations: they regulated apprenticeship
# by hereditary lineage, enforced Igue-tide ritual purity on their members,

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Igue Festival -- the annual purification calendar of divine kingship
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Among Ewuare's reforms after his accession (c.1440) was the codification
# of the Igue, an annual festival of royal renewal still observed in modern
# Benin. The cycle of rites -- Ague, Otue, Ugie Erha Oba, Ugie Oro, Igue
# proper, and the closing Iron-Day -- ran for several weeks in Edo's
# December-January season and required every titled chief in the realm
# (Uzama, Eghaevbo n'Ogbe, Eghaevbo n'Ore, and the Iyase) to attend the
# Oba's palace, present tribute, swear the year's oath of loyalty, and
# undergo the propitiatory sacrifices that renewed the king's mystical
# authority. Igue was simultaneously a calendrical ritual, a tribute
# assessment, and a constitutional review: a chief who failed to appear

# Iyase n'Ode -- the senior town-chief and supreme war-commander
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# The Iyase was the senior of the Eghaevbo n'Ore (the "town chiefs") and the
# constitutional war-commander of the Edo state, leading the Oba's armies

# Ologbo'na -- the resident-governors over the tributary towns
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# As Benin's empire expanded westward and northward in the 15th and 16th
# centuries under Ewuare, Ozolua, and Esigie, the Oba posted Ologbo'na
# (also called Olaen in some Western Edo districts) -- royal residents
# drawn from the palace societies -- to the courts of subjugated Yoruba
# and Edo town-kings, including Akure, Owo, the Ekiti towns, Mahin, and
# the Edo coastal foundations that became Lagos. Each Ologbo'na assessed
# the annual tribute of slaves, ivory, cloth, and salt, attended the
# vassal-king's court as the Oba's representative, and forwarded the
# remittances overland to Benin City. The residents made Benin's tributary
# system functional but their extortion -- famously documented in the
# Akure and Owo records -- generated the chronic resentment that erupted
# in the 17th-century revolts and shrank the empire to its metropolitan
