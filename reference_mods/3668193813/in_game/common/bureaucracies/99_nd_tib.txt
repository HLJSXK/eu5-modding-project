# 1. Kashag -- the council of ministers (kalon) at the heart of Ganden Phodrang
#    governance. Already informally the regent-Desi's advisory body in the
#    seventeenth century, the Kashag was codified by Qianlong's 1751 reforms
#    after the assassination of the lay regent Gyurme Namgyal: four kalon
#    sitting in a single chamber, three lay nobles drawn from the great
#    Lhasa families and one monk-kalon nominated by the Dalai Lama, with
#    no single minister able to act without the others' consent. The
#    Kashag was the empire of paperwork that actually moved the plateau's
#    business, but it embedded the lay aristocracy permanently inside the
#    Ganden Phodrang and proved the most stable institution Tibet built.

# 3. Densa Sum, the Three Pillars -- the three great Gelugpa monasteries

# 4. Mi-tser Estate System -- the allocation of revenue-estates across the
#    plateau into three categories: shung-gyu (government estates worked
#    by tsongpa subjects), choji (monastic estates supporting specific
#    monasteries and their tulku), and gerba (hereditary noble estates
#    held by the great Lhasa houses such as Lhalu, Yuthok, and
#    Phunkhang). Every estate carried obligations of u-lag (forced
#    transport corvee), tsa-tang (army provisions), and produce-render;
#    the mi-tser (literally "human-property") owed labour to whichever
#    estate-holder controlled their natal village. The system kept the

# 5. Dzongpon Magistracy -- the district magistrates of the fifty-three
#    dzong-fortresses that anchored Ganden Phodrang authority across the
#    plateau, from Shigatse and Gyantse in Tsang to Chamdo and Markham in
#    Kham. Each dzong was administered by a paired appointment: one lay
#    dzongpon drawn from the noble registry of the Tse-yik, one
#    monk-dzongpon from the Yiktsang's roll, both rotated on three-year

#    dzong housed the granary, the corvee register, the militia muster,
