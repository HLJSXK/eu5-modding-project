# National Destinies -- Hausa bureaucracies
# Six historically-grounded institutions of the Hausa Bakwai (Kano, Katsina,
# Daura, Gobir, Zaria, Rano, Garun Gabas) and their lesser-seven Banza
# associates, spanning the period from the consolidation of the walled
# city-states (c. 14th century) through the eve of the Sokoto Jihad (1804).
# Author-modifier grants 2 slots; capital adds 1 = 3 total. Player picks 3 of 6.
#
# Sources rest on the Kano Chronicle and the corpus of Hausa institutional
# literature: the bakawa (court titles -- Galadima, Madawaki, Waziri, Magajin
# Gari), the alkali Maliki court system that matured at Katsina under the
# influence of Wangara scholars in the fifteenth century, and the longstanding
# Maguzawa accommodation by which non-Muslim Hausa villages remained tolerated
# tributaries within Muslim-ruled birane.

# 1. Sarauta -- the office-holding system of the Hausa city-state. The Sarki
#    (king) governed through the bakawa, a graded body of titled officers:
#    the Galadima as administrator of the capital and senior counsellor; the
#    Madawaki as commander of the cavalry; the Waziri as chief of the council;
#    the Magajin Gari as ward elder; the Sarkin Yaki as field marshal. Titles
#    were appointive, not strictly hereditary, and the council of nine elders
#    (or "kingmakers") confirmed each new sarki from among eligible princes.

# 4. Wangara Diaspora -- the Soninke-Wangara merchant network whose caravans
#    bound Hausaland to the Niger Bend, the salt mines of Taghaza, and the
#    forest-edge kola markets of Gonja. The Kano Chronicle records that under
#    Sarki Yaji (r. c. 1349-85) the Wangarawa, perhaps as many as one hundred
#    and sixty under Shaikh Abdurrahman Zaite, settled in Kano and brought
#    Islam with them. Their successors anchored Katsina and Kano as sarakuman

# 6. Maguzawa Compact -- the practical accommodation by which non-Muslim
#    Hausa villages, the Maguzawa or "those who fled" (the rural pagans who
#    refused conversion when the Bori cult lost its court patronage), were

#    and their seasonal festivals in exchange for a tribute of grain, cloth,
#    and the obligation of military levy. The compact was unwritten but
