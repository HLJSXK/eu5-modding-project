# expanding emirates to the Sokoto throne. The wazir was the chief
# minister who handled correspondence with emirs, transmitted caliphal
# orders, mediated disputes between emirates, and served as the primary
# interface between the Sarkin Musulmi and the periphery. Gidado dan
# Laima, Bello's first wazir, set the template: a learned scholar-
# bureaucrat from a trusted Toronkawa lineage, dictating rather than

# =====================================================================
# 2. Alkali Court System
#
# The Sokoto Caliphate's judiciary rested on a network of alkalis
# (qadis) trained in Maliki fiqh, the dominant school of West African
# Islamic jurisprudence. Each emirate maintained alkali courts that
# applied sharia in matters of marriage, inheritance, contracts, and
# criminal law -- displacing the customary Hausa law (gargajiya) that
# preceded the jihad. Dan Fodio's Bayan Wujub al-Hijra and Bello's
# Infaq al-Maysur set the legal-theological foundations. The chief
# alkali at Sokoto served as appellate authority for the entire
# caliphate. Clergy and peasants gain access to predictable Islamic
# justice; the old Hausa nobility sees its customary jurisdictions
# stripped away.
# =====================================================================

# =====================================================================
# 3. Jakada Tribute Network
#
# The jakada were resident agents (or rotating envoys) of the caliph
# stationed at each emirate capital. Their role was to collect the
# annual tribute -- the kurdin kasa land tax, the kurdin sarauta
# accession-fee from new emirs, slaves and grain -- and to forward it
# to Sokoto and Gwandu. Modeled loosely on the Abbasid amil and the
# pre-jihad Hausa courier system, jakadas reported on emirate affairs
# and could request caliphal intervention. Effective when their
# loyalty was secured, but corrupt or co-opted jakadas were the most
# common point of fiscal failure: they enriched themselves, the
# emir, or both at the throne's expense.

# =====================================================================
# 4. Toronkawa Emirate Order
#
# Sokoto's emirates were not appointed offices but hereditary
# lineages: each emirate's flag (tuta) was granted to a Fulbe
# clan -- predominantly the Toronkawa (the Torodbe of Senegal-Futa
# Toro origin from which the Fodio family itself descended) and
# allied clans -- with succession passing within the lineage subject
# to caliphal confirmation. The Sullubawa held Kano, the Sungawa held
# Daura, the Mallawa held Katagum. This produced a cohesive Fulbe
# aristocratic order across the caliphate but locked Hausa commoners
# and non-Fulbe groups out of the highest offices, generating the
# resentment that fuelled the 1817 Hadejia revolt and later

# 5. Ribat Frontier Network
#

# Bello's Usul al-Siyasa explicitly ordered the construction of ribats

# scholar, a garrison of muhajirun (refugee-soldiers), and a school

# 6. Maliki Ulama Council
#
# Sokoto was a state founded by scholars. The community of mallams
# (mu'allims) drawn from dan Fodio's circle and his successors --
# Abdullahi dan Fodio's heirs at Gwandu, Bello's at Sokoto, and the
# alkalis they trained -- formed a corporate body of Maliki jurists

# capture. The Bayan Wujub al-Hijra (1806), Kitab al-Farq (1807),

# the caliphate its theological legitimacy but also its harshest
