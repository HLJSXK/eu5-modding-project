#
# Six historically grounded institutions of the central-Javanese

# heartland state -- the kraton-centered apparatus assembled by
# Senapati and Sultan Agung at Kotagede, then expanded under
# Amangkurat at Plered/Kartasura, and finally split by the Treaty

#   4. Imogiri Royal Tomb-Shrine    -- the dynastic burial cult

#   6. Surakarta-Yogyakarta Dual    -- the post-Giyanti partition
#

# picks 3 of 6. IDs are mtm_*; do NOT collide with NUS nus_* IDs.

# =====================================================================
# 1. Patih Lebet / Kepatihan -- the Grand Vizier's Office
#
# At Mataram the patih lebet ("inner patih") was the sultan's chief
# minister, head of the kepatihan central administration that ran
# revenue, justice, and the levy of provincial troops. Beneath him
# stood the patih anom (the understudy patih) and a corps of nayaka
# secretaries. The office was institutionalized by Sultan Agung
# (r.1613-1645) at Kerta and expanded at Kartasura after 1680, where
# the kepatihan complex housed its own audience hall, archive of
# palm-leaf cadasters, and standing seat for petitioners. The patih
# concentrated administrative power in the king's name and short-
# circuited the regional adipati lords -- a centralizing instrument
# that the priyayi aristocracy resented but could not overthrow.

# =====================================================================
# 2. Negaragung-Mancanegara System -- the Four-Ring Concentric Realm
#
# Mataram organized its territory in concentric rings radiating from
# the kraton. The kutagara was the royal city and palace precinct;
# the negaragung ("the great country") was the inner heartland of
# Central Java directly cultivated for the king; the mancanegara
# ("outer countries") were the eastern Javanese provinces governed
# by adipati regents holding apanage land in exchange for prajurit
# levies; and the pasisir was the conquered north coast under
# tumenggung supervision. Each adipati owed annual tribute, corvée
# rotations to the kraton, and a pasewakan audience appearance.
# The system pulled labor and grain inward, but the corvée demands
# bled the peasantry and seeded recurrent uprisings -- Trunajaya
# (1674-1680), the Chinese War (1740), Mangkubumi's revolt (1746).

# =====================================================================
# 3. Pajeksan Land Cadaster -- Sultan Agung's Revenue Survey
#
# Under Sultan Agung, the pajeksan -- a crown corps of revenue
# assessors -- conducted the first systematic land survey of the
# Javanese heartland, recording every sawah rice-field, dry tegalan,
# and house compound on palm-leaf and bark registers held in the
# kepatihan archive. Each cacah (a fiscal household-unit) was
# entered against an adipati's apanage roll, and tax was calculated
# in rice paddy and corvée labour days rather than coin. The system
# vastly increased the king's revenue extraction but bore down hard
# on the cultivator: the cacah quota fell whether or not the harvest
# came, and abusive tax-collectors (the demang and bekel) extracted
# their cut on top of the crown's. The pajeksan registers became the
# documentary backbone of every later Mataram tax dispute.

# =====================================================================
# 4. Imogiri Royal Tomb-Shrine Cult
#
# In 1632 Sultan Agung began constructing the royal burial complex
# at Imogiri ("the misty mountain") in the hills south of his new
# capital, modeled on the older Giriloyo shrines and on the holy
# graves of the Wali Songo. Every Mataram sultan from Agung onward
# was interred at Imogiri, and after 1755 the shrine was shared in
# alternation between Surakarta and Yogyakarta -- the only joint
# institution the partitioned dynasty preserved. Court protocol
# required pilgrimage at coronation, at royal anniversaries, and
# before military campaigns; the abdi-dalem (palace servants) of
# Imogiri governed entry, ritual offerings, and the sacred court
# language used at the tombs. The cult fused Sufi tomb-veneration
# with older Hindu-Javanese ancestor worship, sacralizing the throne
# in a way that even VOC-imposed sultans had to honor.

# =====================================================================
# 5. Bedhaya Court Arts -- the Priyayi Sacred-Dance Regime
#
# Sultan Agung commissioned the Bedhaya Ketawang, a sacred court
# dance for nine female dancers performed only in the kraton's
# audience hall on the king's coronation anniversary; legend held
# that Kanjeng Ratu Kidul, the spirit-queen of the southern sea,
# joined as a tenth invisible dancer. Around this central rite a
# whole institutional apparatus crystallized: the abdi-dalem
# musicians of the gamelan sekaten, the dalang puppet-masters of
# the wayang kulit court repertoire, the empu kraton smiths who
# forged ritual keris, and the priyayi scribes who codified the
# krama and ngoko speech levels. The whole complex elevated the
# priyayi -- the literate aristocratic-administrator class -- as
# the sole licensed transmitters of "high" Javanese culture, but
# the labor of feeding this courtly establishment fell on the
# negaragung peasantry whose corvée and rice-quotas paid for it.

# 6. Surakarta-Yogyakarta Dual Court
#
# The Treaty of Giyanti (13 February 1755) ended the Third Javanese
# War of Succession by formally partitioning Mataram between the
# Susuhunan Pakubuwono III at Surakarta and the new Sultan Hamengku-
# buwono I at Yogyakarta, each holding half the negaragung and half
# the mancanegara, each with its own kraton, its own kepatihan, and
# its own pasewakan audience. The partition was followed by the 1757
# split-off of the Mangkunegaran principality at Surakarta and, in
# 1813, the Pakualaman at Yogyakarta -- four princely courts where
# one had stood. Cultural rivalry produced a remarkable artistic
# flowering: Surakarta refined the Yasadipura literary lineage and
# the elegant alus dance style; Yogyakarta cultivated the more
# vigorous gagah style, Sultan Agung's older Bedhaya, and the
# distinct Yogyanese Javanese script. But the dual administration
# doubled the cost of governance, complicated foreign relations,
