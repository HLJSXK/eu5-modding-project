# =====================================================================
# 1. Bakuhan-Taisei -- the Bakufu-Han Dual System
#
# Codified after Sekigahara (1600) and ratified by the 1615 Genna armistice,
# the bakuhan-taisei distributed authority between the central Edo Bakufu
# (the shogun's tent-government) and roughly 270 semi-autonomous han
# governed by daimyo. The shogunate held foreign affairs, coinage, the
# Five Kaido, and direct rule over the Tenryo lands; the daimyo retained
# fiscal and judicial authority within their domains in return for oaths
# of loyalty, military levies, and the corvee assessments of the kokudaka
# rice-yield system. It was the institutional spine of the Edo state.
# =====================================================================

# =====================================================================
# 2. Sankin-Kotai -- the Alternate Attendance System
#
# Formalised by Tokugawa Iemitsu in the 1635 revision of the Buke
# Shohatto, sankin-kotai required every daimyo to maintain a permanent
# residence (yashiki) in Edo housing his wife and heir as de-facto
# hostages, and to alternate his own residence yearly between Edo and
# his domain. The processions (gyoretsu) along the Five Kaido bankrupted
# the great houses, populated Edo with samurai consumers, drove the
# Genroku urban economy, and bound the daimyo to the shogun's eye --
# but at the cost of a permanent peasant tax burden to fund it.

# =====================================================================
# 5. Buke Shohatto -- the Laws for the Military Houses
#
# Promulgated by Tokugawa Hidetada in 1615 and revised by Iemitsu in
# 1635, the Buke Shohatto was a thirteen-article code regulating the
# conduct of the daimyo and the samurai under their command -- limiting
# castle construction, marriage alliances, factional contact, and the
# size of armed retinues. It was read aloud at the start of every
# shogun's reign and bound the samurai class as a legal estate. Where

#
