# =====================================================================
# 1. The Makhzen -- Sharifian Central Administration
#
# Although the term predates them, the Makhzen as the recognisable
# Sharifian state apparatus took shape under the Saadis (1549-1659)
# and was consolidated by Moulay Ismail (1672-1727). Originally
# meaning "warehouse" or treasury, the word came to denote the
# sultan's mobile court with its hajib (chamberlain), wuzara
# (ministers), kuttab (secretaries), and the harka of palace
# regiments. The Makhzen ruled the Bilad al-Makhzen directly while
# the Bilad as-Siba paid only sporadic tribute. Its instruments
# were Sharifian baraka, the tax-farming amins, and a corps of
# tributary qaids; its limit was the Atlas itself.
# =====================================================================

# =====================================================================
# 2. Jaysh Tribes -- Settler-Soldier Confederacies
#
# From the Saadi reorganisation onward, certain tribes were granted
# the rank of jaysh (army): in exchange for hereditary military
# service to the Makhzen they received tax exemption and grants of
# crown land near Fez, Meknes and the Gharb. The Saadis settled the
# Sus-Ouled-Djeorar, Ouled-Mettaa, Zenara and Shebanat in the Gharb
# to keep the northern Banu Hilal in check. Moulay Ismail systematised
# the institution between 1672 and 1700: 3,000 Cheraga, 4,500
# Sherarda and 2,000 Udaya were stationed around Fez, while the
# Jaysh al-Udaya was constituted from four Banu Maqil tribes of
# Sus -- Ulad Jerrar, Ulad Mtaa, Zirara and the Chebanate. Their
# loyalty was personal to the sultan and exclusive of the urban

# =====================================================================
# 3. Qaid Provincial Government -- Bashawiyya
#
# Outside the directly-administered Bilad al-Makhzen, the sultan
# governed through qaids (provincial governors) drawn from the great
# tribal houses -- the Glaoua of Telouet, the Mtougga of Imintanout,
# the Goundafa of the High Atlas, the Rifian Hamadi of Ouezzane.
# A qaid commanded his own goum (tribal levy), collected the
# kharaj (land tax) at his own discretion, retained a fraction
# before remittance, and adjudicated disputes through customary
# law alongside the qadi's sharia court. The bashas of Marrakech,
# Fez and Meknes were the senior qaids, sometimes second only to
# the sultan himself. The system extracted revenue cheaply at the

# =====================================================================
# 4. Karaouine and the Maliki Ulama
#
# Founded in 859 CE by Fatima al-Fihri, the Qarawiyyin mosque-
# university at Fez became the senior institution of Maliki
# jurisprudence in the western Islamic world. Its ulema staffed
# the qadi courts of every Moroccan city, drafted the legal
# opinions (fatwas) on which dynastic legitimacy rested, and
# administered the great waqf endowments of Fez, Meknes and
# Marrakech. The Marinids expanded the network of madrasas in
# the fourteenth century -- Bou Inania, al-Attarine, al-Sahrij --
# producing a literate scholar-judge class with which the
# Sharifian sultans had to negotiate, not command. The ulema's
# bay'a (oath of fealty) was the indispensable accession ritual.

# =====================================================================
# 5. Zawiya Networks -- Sufi Brotherhoods
#
# The Moroccan zawiya was a Sufi lodge built around the tomb of a
# wali (saint), led by a shaykh who claimed silsila (chain of
# transmission) from the founder. The Shadhili tradition seeded
# the order families that mattered politically: the Jazuliyya
# (15th century), the Nasiriyya of Tamegroute (17th century),
# the Wazzaniyya of the Rif (17th century), the Sharqawa of Boujad
# (16th century), the Darqawiyya (18th century), and Ahmad al-Tijani's
# Tijaniyya, settled at Fez from 1789. During the Maraboutic Crisis
# (c.1610-1660), the Dila zawiya in the Middle Atlas controlled
# central Morocco outright until its destruction by Moulay Rashid
# in 1668. Where the Makhzen could not reach, the zawiya kept order,
# arbitrated, fed travellers, and raised the harvest for the saint's
# moussem.

# 6. Sale Corsair Divan
#
# After the 1609 Morisco expulsion drove some 10,000 Andalusi
# refugees onto the lower Bou Regreg, Sale-Rabat became the corsair
# capital of the Atlantic. From 1627 the city governed itself as
# the Republic of Sale, ruled by a divan of twelve to fourteen
# raises (corsair captains) who annually elected a qaid and a
# Grand Admiral; the Dutch renegade Jan Janszoon (Murad Reis)
# held the latter office in 1624. Ten per cent of every prize was
# paid into the divan's treasury for fortifications and bribes.
# Between 1620 and 1630 Sale corsairs took more than a thousand
# European ships. Moulay al-Rashid suppressed the Republic in
# 1668 but absorbed its admiralty -- the Diwan al-Bahr -- as a

# =====================================================================
# 7. Harka -- Mobile Tax Expedition
#
# The harka (literally "movement" or "burning") was the Moroccan
# answer to the unsubduable tribe: the sultan in person, with the
# mahalla -- harem, ministers, palace guard, jaysh cavalry, mountain
# artillery and a baggage train of African slaves -- marched into
# the Bilad as-Siba once or twice a year to extract back-taxes,
# punish revolt and reinstall qaids. The institution dated from
# the Idrissids; Moulay Ismail and his Alaouite successors made
# it routine. A successful harka returned with hostages, levied
# tribute, and a renewed bay'a; an unsuccessful harka cost the
# sultan a campaign season, soldiers to attrition, and his
