# =====================================================================
# 1. Eparchia Strategoi -- the Indo-Greek satrap-governorship
#
# Apollodotus I, Menander I and their successors administered the
# kingdom through eparchies (provinces) headed by strategoi -- military
# governors who combined the Hellenistic strategos office with the
# satrapal authority inherited from Achaemenid and Seleucid practice.
# Numismatic and Karoshthi epigraphic evidence (the "meridarch" and
# "strategos" titles on the Shinkot reliquary, the Taxila copper-plate
# of Patika) confirms Indo-Greek strategoi held both military command
# and civil revenue authority in their districts. Centralizes power in
# the diadochic king at the cost of the hipparch landed cavalry caste.
# =====================================================================

#
# Menander I's patronage of Buddhism is documented in the Milindapanha

# The institution borrowed Ashoka's dhamma-mahamatra office (royal
# officers tasked with promoting and supervising the dhamma) and
# adapted it to the Greco-Buddhist order of Gandhara and the Punjab.

# =====================================================================
# 3. Polis-Asty Council -- Hellenistic civic government in Indian cities
#
# Sagala (Sialkot), Taxila (Sirkap), Pushkalavati and the Indo-Greek
# foundations preserved the polis-asty form of Greek civic government:
# a council (boule) of citizens, elected magistrates (archons,
# astynomoi for market regulation), and a citizen body controlling
# its own coinage and law within the king's hegemony. Hellenistic
# inscriptions from Ai Khanoum and the Greek city-tradition imported
# into Bactria document the institution; the Indo-Greek tetradrachms
# struck on the Attic standard with city-mint marks confirm civic
# minting authority. The council empowered the urban merchant class
# at the structural expense of the rural hipparch nobility.

# 4. Bilingual Chancery -- Greek and Karoshthi dual-script administration
#
# Indo-Greek royal decrees, reliquary dedications and coin legends
# bear bilingual inscriptions in Greek and Karoshthi (the Aramaic-
# derived script used for Gandhari Prakrit). The Heliodorus pillar
# at Besnagar (c. 110 BC), the Reh inscription, and the obverse-
# reverse legends of every Indo-Greek tetradrachm document a working
# chancery that produced parallel Greek and Prakrit official text.
# This bureaucratic apparatus -- scribes trained in two scripts,
# revenue accounts kept in two languages -- gave the Indo-Greek state
# its characteristic literacy reach into both the Greek garrison
# towns and the Indian countryside.
