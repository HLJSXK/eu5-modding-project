# National Destinies -- Mughal Empire bureaucracies
# 8 historically-grounded institutions of the Mughal state (1526-1857), spanning
# the Babur-to-Aurangzeb era of high imperial administration. The auto_modifier
# grants 3 slots; capital adds 1 = 4 effective. Player picks 4 of 8.
#
# Modeled institutions: Mansabdari rank-grade aristocracy (Akbar 1574),
# Dahsala revenue settlement (Todar Mal 1582), Diwan-i-Khalsa crown lands,
# Jagirdari rotating assignment, Mir Bakshi paymaster-general with the
# dagh-and-chehra muster, the Karkhana imperial workshops, the Sadr-us-Sudur
# religious-endowment office, and the Jharokha-i-Darshan public-audience ritual.
# These together define the Akbari-Aurangzebi system that ran the subcontinent
# from Lahore to Bengal, from Kashmir to the Deccan.

# 1. Mansabdari -- Akbar's rank-grade military aristocracy (instituted 1574,
#    refined under Shah Jahan and Aurangzeb). Every officer of the empire
#    held a dual rank: zat (personal salary grade) and sawar (the cavalry
#    contingent he was obliged to maintain). Ranks ran from mansabdar of
#    ten to the great commanders of seven thousand. The system married
#    Persian-Turkic warband traditions to a centrally-paid state and turned
#    the entire nobility into rotatable, transferable, dismissable officers
#    of the throne. Its decay in the late seventeenth century -- bloated
#    paper-rolls, jagir shortages, faction at court -- is the textbook story
#    of late-Mughal collapse.

# 2. Dahsala Revenue Settlement -- Raja Todar Mal's ten-year revenue system
#    (Ain-i-Dahsala, 1582), which fixed the land-tax for a decade on the
#    average crop-yield of the previous ten years, expressed in silver dams
#    and converted to rupees. It was the first attempt anywhere on the
#    subcontinent at a uniform, centrally-audited, cash-based revenue
#    survey, surveyed by the karoris -- crown agents who measured fields
#    in bighas. Predictable assessment broke the bargaining power of the
#    zamindars but left the peasant (raiyat) at the mercy of a rigid
#    schedule when the harvest failed.

# 4. Jagirdari System -- the assignment of revenue-rights over a tract of
#    land (a jagir) to a mansabdar in lieu of cash salary, transferable at
#    the emperor's pleasure (typically every three to four years). Jagirs
#    were never hereditary in the Akbari design; the rotation prevented
#    the nobility from striking local roots. By the late Aurangzeb period
#    the empire had more mansabdars than jagirs to assign -- the bejagiri
#    crisis -- which produced the factionalism that consumed the eighteenth
#    century. The system rewarded the mansabdar nobility while transferring
#    the cost of overextraction to the peasant, who had no incentive to
#    invest in a holding the assignee would soon leave.

# 5. Mir Bakshi -- the paymaster-general of the empire, head of the

# 6. Karkhana Imperial Workshops -- the network of state-run manufactories

#    armour, fine textiles, jewellery, illuminated manuscripts, and the
#    splendid court goods that defined Mughal majesty. The Buyutat-i-Khas
#    administered them under the Khan-i-Saman (master of the household);

# 7. Sadr-us-Sudur -- the empire's chief sadr, supreme judge in matters of
#    Islamic law and overseer of madad-i-maash (subsistence) grants of
#    rent-free land to scholars, sayyids, mosques and madrasas, and after
#    Akbar's controversial reforms also to Hindu temples and Jain ascetics.

# 8. Jharokha-i-Darshan -- the daily public-audience ritual instituted by
#    Akbar at the eastern jharokha (latticed window) of the imperial
#    palace. Every dawn the emperor presented himself to the crowd
#    assembled below, dispensed instant justice on petitions called up
#    from the throng, and demonstrated by his sheer regular visibility
#    that the Padshah was alive, watching, and approachable. Aurangzeb
#    suspended the practice in his eleventh regnal year as un-Islamic
#    quasi-divine display; contemporaries marked the suspension as the
#    moment the throne lost its direct line to the people. The ritual
