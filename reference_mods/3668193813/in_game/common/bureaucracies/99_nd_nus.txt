# Six historically grounded institutions of the pan-archipelagic state, drawn
# from the 1293-1527 Majapahit empire, the 16th-17th century Pasisir port
# polities, the Wali Songo Islamization, and Mataram's inland sultanate
# centralization (Sultan Agung 1613-1645). Auto-modifier grants 2 slots;
# capital adds 1 = 3 total. Player picks 3 of 6.
#
# Each bureaucracy reflects a distinct face of archipelagic governance:
# the Pamget tributary apparatus that bound outer islands to Trowulan,
# the Sumpah Palapa oath as a court ritual of unification, the Pasisir
# coastal-port confederation, the Wali Songo Sufi missionary network,
# the Mataram tumenggung regency system, and the kapitan ethnic-magistrate
# institution that governed plural settlements in the Sultan's name.

# 1. Pamget Tributary Apparatus -- the Negarakertagama (1365) describes
#    Majapahit's outer realm as a graded hierarchy of mancanagara (outer
#    countries) and nusantara (the "outer islands" proper), each bound by
#    pamget -- annual tribute deliveries of gold, slaves, spices, and
#    aromatic woods carried to Trowulan by sea. Bhre cadet-princes from the
#    royal house governed the inner mandala provinces; outer rulers retained
#    their thrones but were inscribed in palm-leaf registers and confirmed
#    each year by royal envoys. The tributary mandala was loose by Mongol or
#    Chinese standards, but extended Majapahit's sphere from the Malay
#    peninsula through Sulawesi to the Moluccas and western New Guinea.

# 2. Sumpah Palapa Court Ritual -- Gajah Mada's 1336 oath, sworn before the
#    young king Hayam Wuruk, that he would taste no spice (palapa) until the
#    archipelago was unified under Majapahit. The oath was institutionalized
#    in the court ceremonial of Trowulan: each Bhre prince and tributary
#    raja swore allegiance during the annual Caitra festival, robed in
#    ranked court dress, with the patih reciting the territorial litany of
#    nusantara. The ritual fused Hindu-Buddhist royal ceremony with a
#    secular doctrine of pan-archipelagic sovereignty -- the foundation
#    stone of every later "Nusantara" rhetoric. By formalizing the king's
#    centrality, it subordinated the great regional kings of Bali, Madura,

# 4. Wali Songo -- the "Nine Saints" of Java, the syncretic Sufi missionaries
#    who from c.1450 to c.1550 wove Islam into Javanese culture through
#    wayang puppet theatre, gamelan music, and the gradual Islamization of
#    the courts of Demak, Cirebon, and Mataram. Sunan Kalijaga, Sunan Bonang,
#    Sunan Giri, and their fellows founded pesantren religious schools,
#    composed Suluk mystical poetry, and converted millions through cultural
#    accommodation rather than conquest. The institution of the wali --
#    venerated tomb-shrines, pesantren teaching networks, and the kyai
#    spiritual lineage they founded -- supplied the religious backbone of
#    Javanese Islam for centuries, but their populist piety threatened the
#    older Hindu-Buddhist priyayi aristocracy whose courts had defined

# 5. Tumenggung Regency System -- Sultan Agung of Mataram (1613-1645)
#    consolidated the Javanese hinterland by replacing the old Bhre cadet-
#    princedoms with tumenggung regents -- crown-appointed officials drawn
#    initially from royal kinsmen and trusted santri commanders, charged
#    with provincial revenue extraction, road corvée, and the levy of
#    prajurit infantry for Mataram's wars (the sieges of Surabaya 1625 and
#    Batavia 1628-1629). The system pulled administrative weight inland from
#    the pasisir coast and imposed a uniform Javanese court culture --
#    Kawi-derived language, krama speech levels, and Solonese court ritual
#    -- across the heartland. Effective at extracting taxes and soldiers,
#    the tumenggung also centralized so much patronage that pasisir

# 6. Kapitan Ethnic Magistracy -- a peculiarly archipelagic institution: in
#    Malacca, Banten, Aceh, Makassar, and the Mataram pasisir, the sultan
#    appointed a kapitan -- "captain", from Portuguese capitão -- to govern
#    each foreign or non-Muslim community under its own customary law. The
#    Kapitan Cina led the Hokkien and Cantonese merchant quarters; the
#    Kapitan Kling spoke for Tamil and Gujarati traders; the Kapitan Melayu
#    or Kapitan Bugis led migrant communities at non-Malay courts. The
#    kapitan registered births, settled disputes, collected the head-tax,
#    and answered for his community's order to the syahbandar harbour-
#    master. The institution underwrote the cosmopolitan port commerce of
#    the archipelago: it freed the sultan from the cost of governing
#    diasporas directly while binding their wealth to the throne, and it
