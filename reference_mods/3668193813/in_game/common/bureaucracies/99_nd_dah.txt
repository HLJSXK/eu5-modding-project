# National Destinies -- Kingdom of Dahomey bureaucracies
# Five historical institutions of the Fon kingdom of Abomey, spanning the Aja
# foundation under Houegbadja (c.1645), the imperial expansion under Agaja
# (r.1718-1740) that conquered Whydah in 1727, the Oyo-tributary century
# (1730-1820), and the Ghezo-Glele-Behanzin reform era (1818-1894). The
# auto-modifier grants 2 slots; Abomey contributes 1 = 3 total. The player
# picks 3 of 5. Each bureaucracy reflects a distinct face of Dahomean rule:
# the Ahosu's two paramount ministers, the Yovogan's slave-coast viceroyalty,
# the Agojie female regiments, the Xwetanu redistributive festival, and the
# Nesuhwe royal-ancestor priesthood.

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Migan and Mehu -- the paramount and vice-paramount ministers of Abomey
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# The Ahosu (king of Dahomey) governed through two paramount ministers: the
# Migan, chief executioner and prime minister responsible for justice and
# internal security, and the Mehu, comptroller of the royal household and
# treasurer responsible for finance and foreign trade. Both offices were held
# by commoners deliberately drawn from outside the royal lineage so they
# would owe everything to the king. Beneath them sat lesser officers -- the
# Yovogan at Whydah, the Sogan in charge of the king's stables, the Tokpo
# minister of agriculture -- but the Migan-Mehu duumvirate ran the day-to-day
# state. Houegbadja institutionalized the pairing in the late seventeenth
# century; Agaja and Tegbessou expanded it; under Ghezo (r.1818-1858) the two
# offices became the engine of Dahomey's military-fiscal recovery.

	}

			subtract = scope:maintenance
		}

		global_nobles_estate_power = 0.10
	}
}

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Yovogan of Whydah -- royal viceroy of the Slave Coast trading port
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# After Agaja conquered the kingdom of Whydah in 1727, Dahomey absorbed the
# Atlantic slave-trading entrepot of Glehue (Ouidah) and posted a permanent
# royal viceroy -- the Yovogan, literally "chief of the white men" -- to
# govern the port and tax every slave, cowrie, and bale of cloth that passed
# through the European factories of the French, Portuguese, English, and
# Dutch. The Yovogan held a monopoly on negotiating prices with European
# captains, levied a per-head export duty, and remitted gold and goods to
# Abomey on a fixed schedule. The office made the slave trade a state
# enterprise; under Ghezo it pivoted to palm oil after British anti-slavery
# patrols closed off the Atlantic market in the 1840s.

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Agojie -- the female warrior corps of the royal household
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# The Agojie (Mino, "our mothers"), known to Europeans as the Dahomey Amazons,
# were a corps of female soldiers attached to the royal palace. Originating
# under Queen Hangbe in the early eighteenth century as elephant huntresses
# and palace guards, they were formalized under Agaja and expanded under
# Ghezo into a standing regiment that grew to roughly six thousand troops --
# perhaps a third of Dahomey's full military strength. Recruited from
# captives, slaves, and even free Fon women whose fathers volunteered them,
# the Agojie were technically wives of the king and lived inside the palace
# walls in barrack quarters under their own officers. Their separation from
# the male nobility's cavalry meant the throne possessed a permanent striking
# force that no chief could divert; they covered themselves in glory at the
# defense of Cana and Cotonou in the Franco-Dahomean wars of 1890 and 1892.

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Xwetanu -- the Annual Customs of Abomey
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# The Xwetanu, the kingdom's Annual Customs, was a weeks-long festival held
# each dry season at Abomey that combined ancestor veneration, military
# review, judicial assize, trade fair, and the distribution of royal gifts
# to the assembled chiefs and ministers. The Ahosu personally redistributed
# cowries, cloth, captives, and titles to subordinate officers; offerings
# of slaves and cattle were made at the tombs of the previous kings; new
# decrees were proclaimed and disputed succession claims arbitrated. Foreign
# embassies presented their tribute and the Yovogan reported on Whydah's
# accounts. The festival's scale rose with the kingdom's revenues -- under
# Ghezo and Glele the Customs lasted six weeks and consumed the surplus of
# the entire year -- and its abolition by the French in 1894 was a symbolic
# end of the kingdom as much as a fiscal one.

# administered by a permanent priesthood at Abomey under the Tassinon (or
# Tasinon), the chief priest of the royal-line spirits. Each deceased king
# entered the Nesuhwe pantheon and received continuous offerings at his
# tomb-shrine; living princes and princesses had Nesuhwe sodalities of
# initiated dancers and diviners attached to them. The Quenum diviners
# performed Fa (Ifa) consultation on royal business. The Nesuhwe stood
# alongside but distinct from the wider Vodun (vodu) pantheon -- Mawu-Lisa
# the supreme androgyne, Legba the trickster gatekeeper, Sakpata the earth
# and smallpox lord, Hevioso the thunderer -- whose priests served the
