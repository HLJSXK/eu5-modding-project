# National Destinies -- Persia/Iran (IRA) bureaucracies
# Seven historically grounded institutions of the Persian state, spanning the
# late-Timurid into Safavid, post-Abbasid reform, and Qajar appanage eras.
# The auto_modifier grants 3 slots; capital adds 1 = 4 effective. Player picks
# 4 of 7. Two pairs of these stand in deliberate institutional tension --
# Qizilbash uymaq versus the Ghulam household, and the unitary Divan-i-A'la
# versus the Qajar Mamalik-i-Mahrusa appanage system -- modeling the central
# choice of every Persian throne: who actually wields the realm.

# 1. Divan-i-A'la -- the supreme chancery of the realm, headed by the
#    Vazir-i-Azam (Grand Vizier) under Shah Tahmasp and elaborated to its
#    classical form by Shah Abbas I after his Isfahan transfer of 1598.
#    The Divan-i-A'la presided over the diwans of finance (mostowfi), of
#    army (lashkar-nivis), of religious endowment (sadarat), and of the
#    royal household (khassa), with the Grand Vizier hearing petitions
#    each morning and signing edicts under the royal tughra. A capable
#    vizier -- Mirza Salman, Itimad al-Dawla -- could make a young shah's
#    reign; an empty vizierate (the long Qajar interludes) left ministries
#    feuding and revenue uncollected. The chancery existed to bind the
#    diwans into a single throne, which is why the umara hated it.

# 2. Sadr-us-Sudur -- the chief sadr of the realm, supreme office of the
#    Twelver Shia clergy, formalized by Shah Ismail I after his 1501
#    declaration of Twelver Shi'ism as state religion and split by Shah
#    Tahmasp into the Sadr-i-Khassa (overseeing royal-domain endowments)
#    and the Sadr-i-Mamalik (overseeing those of the realm at large). The
#    sadr controlled the waqf properties that financed the madrasas of
#    Najaf, Karbala, and Isfahan, appointed Shaykhs al-Islam in the great
#    cities, and supervised the conversion of a population that was

# 3. Ghulam Household Corps -- Shah Abbas I's standing army of the
#    royal slaves, instituted around 1599 with the conversion and
#    enrollment of Caucasian Christian boys -- Georgians, Armenians,
#    Circassians -- captured or tributed from the Caucasus, raised in
#    the palace, and assigned offices and revenue grants as the shah's
#    personal household. Modeled on the Ottoman devshirme and Mamluk
#    iqta, the ghulam corps gave the throne for the first time a body
#    of officers and governors who owed nothing to the Turcoman uymaq
#    chiefs and everything to the Padshah. By Shah Abbas's death in 1629
#    ghulams held the great governorships of Fars, Azerbaijan, and

# 4. Qizilbash Uymaq Confederacy -- the seven (later nine) Turcoman
#    tribes -- Shamlu, Ustajlu, Takkalu, Rumlu, Afshar, Qajar, Zulqadr,
#    and others -- whose cavalry put Shah Ismail on the throne at
#    Sharur in 1501 and won at Tabriz, Hoy, and the long campaigns
#    against the Uzbeks and Ottomans. The Qizilbash ("red-heads",
#    for their twelve-pleated taj of Shi'i devotion) provided the
#    great offices of state -- the Qurchi-bashi, the lala (royal

#    I built the ghulam corps precisely to escape this dependence;

# 5. Tofangchi Musket Corps -- the standing musketeer infantry instituted

# 6. Mostowfi-ul-Mamalik -- Chief Accountant of the Realm, the head of
#    the Diwan-i-Mostowfi, who maintained the registers of every iqta
#    and tuyul assignment, audited the provincial mostowfis under him,
#    and presented the annual budget (dastur al-amal) to the shah and
#    the Grand Vizier. The office traced through Buyid and Seljuq
#    practice into the Safavid state, where Mirza Salman Jaberi's

#    office to attempt fiscal reform against the appanages of the
