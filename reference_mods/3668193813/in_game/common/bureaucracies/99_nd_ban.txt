# National Destinies -- Banten Sultanate bureaucracies
# Five historically-grounded institutions of the Banten Sultanate (1527-1813),
# rooted in the Surasoan Karaton at Banten Lama (the Sultan's walled palace
# complex begun under Maulana Hasanuddin in 1552 and elaborated through the
# reigns of Yusuf, Maulana Muhammad, and Abulmafakhir Mahmud Abdulkadir);
# the cosmopolitan syahbandar customs administration that ran Banten port
# with separate harbormasters for Chinese, Gujarati, Malay, and (after 1596)
# European traders; the Tumenggung-led tributary apparatus that channelled
# inland Lampung pepper to the port through a chain of military governors;
# the Sultan-Caliph's ulama hierarchy built on the 1638 Meccan firman that
# granted the Banten ruler the title sultan and the Cirebon-derived
# pesantren scholarship that came with it; and the polyglot mercenary
# corps -- Buginese, Macassarese, Balinese, slave-soldiers from Madagascar
# and even a few European deserters -- that Sultan Ageng Tirtayasa
# (r. 1651-1683) hired to break the rural priyayi cavalry monopoly during
# his reformed-army reign.
#
# Auto-modifier grants 2 slots; capital adds 1 = 3 effective. Player picks
# 3 of 5. All IDs prefixed with `ban_` to avoid cross-file collisions.

# 1. Surasoan Karaton -- the Sultan's walled palace complex at Banten Lama,
#    a kraton in the Demak-Cirebon Javanese style enclosing the dalem
#    (private residence), the pendopo (audience hall), and the alun-alun
#    (ceremonial square) where the Sultan reviewed his guards on Friday.
#    Begun under Maulana Hasanuddin (r. 1552-1570) and successively rebuilt
#    and fortified -- the Italian renegade Hendrik Lucasz Cardeel raised
#    the Tasik Ardi reservoir and the European-style brick walls in the
#    1670s for Sultan Ageng. The karaton was the institutional theatre of
#    Bantenese kingship: the Pancagati council of five elders, the chief
#    qadi, the syahbandars, and the visiting envoys all conducted their
#    business under its roofs, and the ritual seclusion of the Sultan
#    behind successive courtyards conferred the legitimacy that distinguished
#    the ruler of Banten from any provincial Javanese lord.

# 2. Syahbandariate of Banten -- the multi-ethnic harbormaster system that
#    administered Banten port from the late 16th century to the 1682 VOC
#    occupation. Following the Malacca-Pasai pattern, the Sultan appointed
#    a separate syahbandar for each of the major foreign communities
#    trading at Banten: a Chinese syahbandar over the Pecinan quarter, a
#    Gujarati or Keling syahbandar for South Asian traders, a Malay
#    syahbandar for archipelago shippers, and (after the Dutch and English
#    arrivals of 1596 and 1602) European factors whose factories paid
#    fees to the throne. Each syahbandar collected anchorage and customs
#    in his own jurisdiction, settled disputes within his community by
#    its own law, and answered jointly to the Sultan's chief minister.
#    The arrangement made Banten a free port in the Banten-Aceh-Makassar
#    sense -- not free of duties, but free of any one community's
#    monopoly -- and was the institutional reason the pepper of Lampung
#    reached buyers from a dozen nations through a single roadstead.

# 3. Tumenggung Tributary System -- the chain of military governors,
#    titled Tumenggung in the Mataram-Demak Javanese pattern, who held
#    the inland pepper-producing districts of Lampung (across the Sunda
#    Strait on south Sumatra) and the Banten interior on behalf of the
#    Sultan. Each Tumenggung commanded a small force of priyayi cavalry
#    and infantry, supervised the pepper plantations through Lampung
#    village headmen (the pasirah), forwarded the pepper as tribute to
#    Banten port for the syahbandars to sell, and retained a share for
#    his own household. The system gave Banten its near-monopoly on
#    archipelago pepper from c. 1620 to the VOC's 1682 imposition of a
#    contract-monopoly. It also fed the centrifugal tendencies that
#    plagued every Javanese coastal sultanate: the Tumenggungs were
#    hereditary, jealous of their bawahan villages, and quick to side
#    with rebellious princes during succession crises.
