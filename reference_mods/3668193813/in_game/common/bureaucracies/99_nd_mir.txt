# National Destinies -- Imerina (Madagascar) bureaucracies
# 5 historically-grounded institutions of the unified Malagasy state. The
# core is the Merina kingdom of the central highlands (Andrianampoinimerina
# 1787-1810, Radama I 1810-1828, Ranavalona I 1828-1861), supplemented with
# institutional patterns that long predate Imerina unification: the Hova-
# Andriana caste hierarchy, the Sampy keepers of the royal idols, the
# fokonolona village council, the fanompoana corvee, and Jean Laborde's
# industrial complex at Mantasoa. Auto-modifier grants 2 slots; capital
# adds 1 = 3 effective. Player picks 3 of 5.
#
# Scope note: MIR is a Level 2 formable. Madagascar's institutional life
# under Andrianampoinimerina and his successors was unusually layered --
# the kingdom rested simultaneously on consensual village assembly, on a
# rigid endogamous caste, on sacred ancestral relics, on a mass corvee that
# could be commuted to taxes, and on a foreign-built industrial complex that
# manufactured cannons in the highlands. Five bureaucracies are needed to
# capture the spread; three slots force a real choice between consensus,
# coercion, ritual, hierarchy, and industry.

# 1. Fokonolona -- the village council, the foundational political unit of
#    Imerina. Andrianampoinimerina formalised it during his reunification
#    of the highlands after the 77 years of civil war ending in 1787:
#    village elders and notables sat in assembly to enact local regulations,
#    settle disputes, allocate communal labour, and police taboos. Local
#    authority was real -- the fokonolona handled rice-paddy rotations,
#    irrigation maintenance, and kabary-mediated disputes between neighbours
#    -- but it sat under royal directives, with the king's orders read out
#    in the assembly square. The fokonolona pre-dates the Merina kingdom and
#    survived all its monarchs; nineteenth-century French observers called
#    it the most genuinely participatory African institution they encountered.
mir_fokonolona_bureaucracy = {
	implementation_price = price:implement_bureaucracy_price

	removal_price = price:remove_bureaucracy_price

		multiply = 0.002
	}

	potential = {
		has_or_had_tag = MIR
		has_game_rule = nd_bureaucracies_enabled
	}

	allow = {}

	estates_that_like = {
		peasants_estate
		burghers_estate
	}
	estates_that_dislike = {
		nobles_estate
	}

	# Recognising the village fokonolona as a chartered jurisdiction lets
	# peasant elders sit in deliberation alongside merina notables -- the
	# rural countryside governs itself through assembly rather than under
	# Andriana magistrates appointed from Antananarivo.
	neutral_modifier = {
		peasants_estate_can_participate_in_parliament = yes
	}

	# Funded -- elders convened, the kabary-square at Andohalo and a
	# thousand provincial commons kept clear, royal heralds reading out
	# decrees in the assembly -- the fokonolona reaches every village.
	# Communal labour gets allocated, irrigation canals get maintained,
	# disputes get settled before they become feuds, and the rural pop
	# rises into Hova standing through the assembly's recognition.
	positive_modifier = {
		scale = { value = scope:maintenance }
		global_max_rural_control = 0.03
		peasants_estate_target_satisfaction = small_permanent_target_satisfaction
		global_pop_promotion_speed = 0.05
	}

	# Neglected, the fokonolona's rulings go unenforced, the irrigation
	# canals silt up, the rice-paddy rotations collapse into private
	# claims, and the Andriana magistrates step in -- but their justice is
	# arbitrary and their tributes heavier than the assembly's allocations.
	negative_modifier = {
		scale = {
			value = 1
			subtract = scope:maintenance
		}
		nobles_estate_satisfaction_decay = 0.015
		global_pop_demotion_speed = 0.10
	}
}

# 2. Fanompoana -- the corvee labour system, theoretically a personal duty
#    owed to the sovereign in lieu of monetary tax. Under Andrianampoinimerina
#    it was mostly seasonal and modest. Under Radama I (from c. 1820) iron-
#    workers, gunsmiths, and Merina youth apprenticed to European artisans
#    fell under permanent fanompoana to supply the standing army. Under
#    Ranavalona I (1828-1861) it metastasised -- Mantasoa's industrial works
#    employed twenty thousand forced labourers, the army was raised to
#    twenty-to-thirty thousand by conscription-as-fanompoana, and the
#    population of Imerina is estimated to have halved between 1833 and
#    1839 under the combined weight of corvee, war, and famine. The system
#    remains a defining institution of the Merina state -- a tax in days,
#    not coin, paid by every commoner the crown could reach.

		multiply = 0.003

	potential = {
		has_or_had_tag = MIR
		has_game_rule = nd_bureaucracies_enabled
	}

	allow = {}

	estates_that_like = {
		crown_estate
	}
	estates_that_dislike = {
		peasants_estate
		nobles_estate

	# Replacing the head-tax with a labour-tax converts every commoner's

	}

	# Funded -- the rolls accurate, the relays of Hova foremen paid, the

	# Neglected, the fanompoana decays into local extortion -- foremen sell
	# exemptions, Hova elders bribe their neighbours' sons to be taken

	# fall and the countryside grows hungry.
	negative_modifier = {

			value = 1
			subtract = scope:maintenance
		}
		peasants_estate_satisfaction_decay = 0.025
		global_pop_food_consumption = 0.05
	}

# 3. Andriana Caste Hierarchy -- the six-tier endogamous Andriana nobility
#    of the Merina, ordered Andriantompokoindrindra, Andriamasinavalona,
#    Andriandranando, Zazamarolahy, Andriamamilazabe, Andriantompokoindrindra
#    -- each sub-stratum with its own inherited occupation, marriage rules,
#    burial rights, and tomb privileges. Andriana, Hova, and Andevo were
#    forbidden to intermarry; Andevo were considered olona maloto (impure)
#    and could not own land. Beneath the Andriana sat the Hova free
#    commoners, who managed rice and crop lands as owners and traders. The
#    caste system was the architecture of Merina political life: it
#    determined who could hold office, who could trade, who could bear arms,
#    and who could be buried in the family tomb. Andrianampoinimerina
#    codified its rules; Ranavalona I defended them against missionary
#    egalitarianism; the French abolished the legal slavery underpinning
#    the system in 1896.

# 4. Mpitahiry Sampy -- the keepers of the twelve royal Sampy, the great
#    ancestral idols (Kelimalaza, Ramahavaly, Manjakatsiroa and others)
#    inherited and consolidated by Andriamasinavalona's successors. Each
#    sampy had its own keeper-lineage, its own sacrificial calendar, and its
#    own oracular voice; collectively they conferred hasina (sacred power)
#    on the king and validated his decrees. Andrianampoinimerina relied on
#    their oracles before campaigns; Ranavalona I, after expelling the
#    missionaries in 1835, made the cult of the royal sampy state policy and
#    revived the Fandroana royal-bath ceremony in their presence. Radama I,
#    by contrast, partly tolerated the missionaries and let the keepers'
#    political weight slip. The keepers were power-brokers in their own
#    right -- consulted before war, before treaty, before tax -- and the

# 5. Mantasoa Industrial Complex -- the state manufactory built by Jean
#    Laborde for Ranavalona I from 1837 onwards at Mantasoa, southeast of
#    Antananarivo. Laborde, a French castaway with engineering training,
#    organised some twenty thousand fanompoana labourers to build the dam,
#    foundries, kilns, and workshops; on completion roughly 1,200 permanent
#    workmen produced cannon, swords, gunpowder, bricks, tiles, glass,
#    porcelain, paper, silk, soap, candles, and sealing-wax for the royal
#    monopoly. It was the first industrial complex in sub-Saharan Africa
#    raised by an indigenous polity. Production ceased when Laborde was
#    exiled to Reunion in 1855 and was never restarted at scale; the kilns
