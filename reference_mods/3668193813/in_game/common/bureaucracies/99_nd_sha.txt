# National Destinies -- Shan States bureaucracies
# 4 historically-grounded institutions of the Tai/Shan highland polity, drawn
# from the Mong Mao confederation (14th-15th c.) through the surviving sawbwa
# system that persisted under Burmese suzerainty into the colonial era. The
# auto_modifier grants 2 slots; the capital adds 1 = 3 effective. Player picks
# 3 of 4. SHA is moderately documented but each of these institutions is
# attested in Tai/Shan ethnohistory and the chronicles of Hsenwi, Hsipaw,
# Kengtung, and the Mong Mao polity.
#
# Modeled institutions: Saopha (Sky-Lord) hereditary court, Sawbwa Council
# confederation under a paramount lord, Pongyi-Kyaung village-monastery
# Theravada patronage network, and the muang irrigation assembly that
# managed terraced wet-rice cultivation in the highland valleys.

# 1. Saopha (Sky-Lord) Hereditary Court -- the foundational institution of
#    every Shan/Tai mong. Each principality was ruled by a saopha (Tai
#    "sao hpa" = lord of the sky), claiming descent from a celestial
#    progenitor and bound to his subjects by ritual oath rather than
#    bureaucratic appointment. The saopha presided from the haw (palace
#    enclosure) at the centre of his mong, surrounded by a court of
#    amat ministers, panyi (Buddhist chaplains), and the maha-devi
#    chief consort whose lineage often outranked his own. The system
#    gave the Shan polity cohesion at the level of each principality
#    but deliberately resisted central absorption -- each saopha was a
#    sovereign in miniature, never a governor.

	implementation_price = price:implement_bureaucracy_price

# 2. Sawbwa Council Confederation -- the periodic assembly of saophas
#    under a paramount lord, attested most clearly under Mong Mao in the
#    14th and early 15th centuries when Sao Hkun Hkam and his successors
#    drew the surrounding mong into a tributary federation, and again in
#    the 16th-century Hsenwi paramountcy. The council met to coordinate
#    military levies, settle inter-mong succession disputes, and present
#    unified embassies to Yunnan, Ava, and Lanna. Membership was by
#    invitation of the paramount but every saopha attended in person --
#    no proxies -- which kept each lord politically engaged in the
#    confederation but gave the paramount no power to depose his peers.

# 3. Pongyi-Kyaung Sangha Patronage -- the village-monastery network
#    through which Theravada Buddhism, arriving in the Shan highlands
#    by the 14th century via Lanna and Burmese contact, displaced the
#    older Satsana Phi spirit-religion as the religion of the elite and
#    progressively of the peasantry. The pongyi (Burmese "great-glory" =
#    senior monk) ran a kyaung (monastery) in every village of any size,
#    teaching boys to read Pali and Shan script, copying manuscripts,
#    and adjudicating local disputes by the dhamma. Royal patronage by
#    the saophas funded shwedagons and ordination platforms; the result
#    was the highest male literacy rate in mainland Southeast Asia, but
#    the conversion progressively alienated the animist Wa, Palaung, and
#    Lahu hill peoples who lived under tributary submission.

# 4. Muang Irrigation Assembly -- the muang itself was originally a
#    hydraulic concept: a Tai polity defined by its shared irrigation
#    works (mai-faai weirs and lai-muang feeder canals) watering the
#    terraced wet-rice valleys of the Shan plateau and northern Lanna.
#    The keng-muang (assembly of cultivators), under a fai-muang water-
#    master appointed by the saopha but elected from the village heads,
#    set the planting calendar, allocated water shares, and conscripted
#    the corvee labour to repair the weirs each pre-monsoon. The system
#    delivered reliable rice surpluses to support the haw and the
#    sangha, but it constrained the saopha's power to extract beyond
#    the customary share and gave the cultivator a recognised legal
#    standing the lowland Burmese peasant did not enjoy.
