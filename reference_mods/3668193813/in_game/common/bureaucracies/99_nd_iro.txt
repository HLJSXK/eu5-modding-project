# National Destinies -- Haudenosaunee (IRO) bureaucracies.
# Six historically grounded institutions of the Iroquois Confederacy:
# the Grand Council of fifty sachems at Onondaga, where the league
# convened beneath the Tree of Peace; the Clan Mother system by which
# matrons of the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, and Seneca selected
# and could recall their nation's sachems; the Wampum Record Office of
# the appointed faithkeepers who held the belts as binding legal and
# diplomatic memory; the Covenant Chain protocol by which the
# Confederacy bound itself in linked alliance to the Dutch (1613,
# Kaswenta / Two Row Wampum), then to the English (1677 Albany, then
# annual renewals at Onondaga and Albany through 1755); the Mourning
# War apparatus of captive-taking and adoption that replenished
# population during the seventeenth-century Beaver Wars; and the False
# Face Society medicine lodges that administered healing ceremony and
# the seasonal cycle of feasts.
#
# Auto-modifier (main_menu/common/auto_modifiers/99_nd_iro.txt) grants
# 2 slots after formation; the capital adds 1 = 3 effective. Player
# picks 3 of 6. All IDs are prefixed `iro_` to avoid cross-file id
# collisions across the bureaucracy directory.

# 1. Grand Council at Onondaga -- the deliberative apex of the
#    Confederacy. Fifty hereditary sachem titles, distributed unevenly
#    among the Five Nations (Mohawk 9, Oneida 9, Onondaga 14, Cayuga
#    10, Seneca 8), met around the central fire at Onondaga whenever
#    questions of war, peace, or league business were laid before them.
#    The Onondaga Tadodaho presided; the Mohawks and Senecas sat as the
#    Elder Brothers on one side of the fire, the Oneidas and Cayugas as
#    the Younger Brothers on the other, and the Onondagas as Firekeepers
#    held the casting voice in case of deadlock. Consensus was required
#    for league action; a single nation could refuse and the matter
#    rested. The institution was codified in the Kaianere'ko:wa, the
#    Great Law of Peace, recited from wampum at every condolence.

# 2. Clan Mother Council -- the matrilineal foundation of Haudenosaunee
#    governance. Each of the three (later nine) clans -- Wolf, Bear,
#    Turtle, then Deer, Beaver, Hawk, Eel, Snipe, and Heron -- held
#    sachem titles in trust through its senior matrons. The clan mother
#    nominated the holder of each title, presented him to the council
#    for confirmation, and could recall him for misconduct, cowardice,
#    or failure to heed the women's counsel. She also held the right to
#    raise mourning wars to replace deceased clan members and to forbid
#    a war her warriors proposed by withholding the moccasins, dried
#    corn, and parched meal needed for the campaign. The institution

# 3. Wampum Record Office -- the keepers of the league's binding memory.
#    Wampum, the polished beads of whelk and quahog drilled into long
#    strings and woven into pictorial belts, served as the legal, ritual,
#    and diplomatic record of the Five Nations. The Hiawatha Belt
#    recorded the founding alliance of the league; the Two Row Wampum
#    recorded the Dutch covenant of 1613; the various Covenant Chain

#    faithkeepers (atatarho hone'ratiyo, "those who keep the belts")

#    and trained their successors in the texts. No matter what passed

# 4. Covenant Chain Protocol -- the diplomatic system that bound the
#    Confederacy to colonial powers from 1613 onward. Beginning with
#    the Two Row Wampum (Kaswenta) negotiated with Dutch traders at
#    Tawagonshi in 1613, the Confederacy maintained that its alliances
#    with European powers were "two parallel rows" -- Indigenous and
#    European peoples travelling the same river in separate vessels,
#    neither steering the other's. The Dutch chain passed to the
#    English at New York's surrender in 1664 and was renewed at Albany
#    in 1677 as the formal Covenant Chain, "polished" annually by
#    Confederacy delegations and crown commissioners through 1755.
#    The Confederacy's professional diplomats -- pre-eminently the

# 5. Mourning War Apparatus -- the institutional machinery of captive-
#    taking and adoption that sustained Haudenosaunee population through
#    the Beaver Wars (c. 1640-1701) and the smallpox attrition of the
#    seventeenth century. The clan mother of a deceased Haudenosaunee
#    raised the war by laying down the dead's name on the longhouse
#    floor; the war chiefs gathered volunteers, mounted a campaign
#    against an external nation -- Huron, Erie, Petun, Susquehannock,
#    Algonquin, Shawnee -- and returned with captives. The captives
#    were ritually processed: some executed in formal blood-revenge,
#    others adopted in the names of the deceased, given their longhouse
#    place and their relations, and integrated as full Haudenosaunee.
#    By 1660 perhaps a third of the Confederacy's population was adopted
