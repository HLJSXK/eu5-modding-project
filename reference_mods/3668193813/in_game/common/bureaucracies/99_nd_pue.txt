# National Destinies -- United Pueblo (PUE) bureaucracies.
# Five historically grounded institutions of the Pueblo world:
# the cacique / war captain dual office that split inside ritual-political
# authority from outside martial authority; the kiva ceremonial chamber
# that served as both religious lodge and political assembly; the
# Summer / Winter (Tewa) and Squash / Turquoise (Hopi) moiety system that
# alternated public authority by season and kept any single faction from
# capturing the pueblo year-round; the knotted-cord runner network that
# Po'pay used in 1680 to coordinate the Pueblo Revolt from Taos to Hopi
# in eight days, here preserved as a permanent inter-pueblo coordination
# institution; and the Pecos / Cuartelejo plains-trade intermediary
# tradition by which eastern pueblos brokered maize, cotton, and turquoise
# for Apache, Comanche, and Wichita bison hides at the autumn fairs.
#
# The pan-Pueblo state PUE represents is thinly attested historically --
# the Revolt confederation lasted twelve years (1680-1692) -- but each
# component institution is well documented. Auto-modifier
# (main_menu/common/auto_modifiers/99_nd_pue.txt) grants 2 slots after
# formation; the capital adds 1 = 3 effective. Player picks 3 of 5. All
# IDs prefixed `pue_` to avoid id collisions across the bureaucracy
# directory.

# 1. Cacique and War Captain Dual Office -- the structural division of
#    Pueblo authority into an "inside" ritual-political chief (the cacique
#    or tlatocan, in Tewa the po'kwingeh) and an "outside" military chief
#    (the war captain, in Tewa the t'owa e'). The cacique held office for
#    life, was confirmed by the religious societies, and concerned himself
#    with the ceremonial calendar, the planting and harvest, and the inner
#    peace of the pueblo. The war captain was selected annually (often by
#    the cacique with the moieties' counsel), held the right of warriors
#    and the policing of the village, and took the field in the season of
#    raid against Apache, Navajo, and later Spanish encroachers. The two
#    offices checked each other -- the war captain could not undertake
#    war without the cacique's concurrence and the ritual sanction of the
#    societies, and the cacique could not police the village without the
#    war captain's hand.
pue_cacique_war_captain_bureaucracy = {
	implementation_price = price:implement_bureaucracy_price
	maintenance_price = price:maintain_bureaucracy_price
	removal_price = price:remove_bureaucracy_price
	maintenance_price_modifier = {
		value = country_economical_base
		multiply = 0.003
	}

	potential = {
		has_or_had_tag = PUE
		has_game_rule = nd_bureaucracies_enabled
	}

	allow = {}

	estates_that_like = {
		clergy_estate
		crown_estate
	}
	estates_that_dislike = {
		nobles_estate
	}

	# The dual office is the structural fact of Pueblo politics: a cacique
	# seated for life by the religious societies, a war captain renewed
	# each year by the moieties, and neither able to act without the other.
	neutral_modifier = {
		nobles_estate_blocked_from_leading_military = yes
	}

	# Cacique tending the ceremonial calendar and the inner peace of the
	# pueblo, war captain commanding the warriors and the village police,
	# the religious societies confirming both -- the pueblo governs itself
	# by the dual hand and is not captured by any single ambitious lineage.
	positive_modifier = {
		scale = { value = scope:maintenance }
		clergy_estate_target_satisfaction = small_permanent_target_satisfaction
		stability_cost_efficiency = 0.1
		monthly_legitimacy = 0.1
	}

	# The dual office collapses into rival monopolies: a war captain who
	# refuses to step down at the year's end, a cacique who undertakes
	# secular policing in his own name, the religious societies splitting
	# their endorsement between contenders -- the pueblo's checks on its
	# own leadership fail and the great clans assert hereditary claims
	# the dual office was meant to forestall.
	negative_modifier = {
		scale = {
			value = 1
			subtract = scope:maintenance
		}
		nobles_estate_satisfaction_decay = 0.01
		monthly_tribal_cohesion = -0.05
	}
}

# 2. Kiva Council Chamber -- the subterranean ceremonial room that served
#    as the political-religious heart of every pueblo. Entered by ladder
#    through the roof opening that represented the sipapu of emergence,
#    the kiva housed the religious societies (the kachina cult, the
#    medicine societies, the warrior fraternities) and was where the
#    cacique convened the village's elders for matters of consequence:
#    the calendar of feasts, the assignment of irrigation water, the
#    judgement of grave wrongs, the ratification of war. Each pueblo had
#    several kivas, divided by moiety or by society membership; the great
#    kivas of Chaco-era construction (Pueblo Bonito, Aztec, Casa Rinconada)
#    held more than a hundred. The kiva was simultaneously sacred precinct,
#    council chamber, weaving workshop, and men's lodge -- the polity and

# 3. Moiety Dual System -- the social-political division of each pueblo
#    into two complementary halves that alternated authority by season.
#    Among the Tewa pueblos these were the Summer People (kossa, the
#    "winter-clowns" of dry farming and the warm half-year) and the
#    Winter People (kwirana, the "summer-clowns" of cold, hunt, and the
#    cold half-year), each with its own cacique, its own kiva, and its
#    own calendar of feasts. Among the Hopi the analogous division ran
#    Squash / Turquoise. Authority for the public business of the pueblo
#    -- assigning fields, calling work parties, mounting hunts -- passed
#    from one moiety to the other at the equinoxes, so that no single
#    faction held the village year-round. The moieties were exogamous:
#    a Summer man married a Winter woman, and the children took the
#    mother's moiety, weaving the village together by kinship while
#    keeping its public faces alternating.
pue_moiety_dual_system_bureaucracy = {
	implementation_price = price:implement_bureaucracy_price
	maintenance_price = price:maintain_bureaucracy_price
	removal_price = price:remove_bureaucracy_price
	maintenance_price_modifier = {
		value = country_economical_base
		multiply = 0.003
	}

	potential = {
		has_or_had_tag = PUE
		has_game_rule = nd_bureaucracies_enabled
	}

	allow = {}

	estates_that_like = {
		tribes_estate
		peasants_estate
	}
	estates_that_dislike = {
		crown_estate
	}

	# Each pueblo divided into Summer and Winter (or Squash and Turquoise),
	# the public office passing between moieties at the equinoxes, the
	# kivas paired and the feasts alternating -- the village holds itself
	# together by exogamy and rotates its leadership by the calendar.
	neutral_modifier = {
		tribes_estate_target_satisfaction = small_permanent_target_satisfaction
	}

	# Summer and Winter caciques each in their season, work parties called
	# by the moiety in office, marriage arranged across the line and the
	# children claiming the mother's half -- factional capture of the
	# pueblo is structurally impossible, food stores swell with the
	# coordinated work cycle, and rural unrest finds no purchase.
	positive_modifier = {
		scale = { value = scope:maintenance }
		peasants_estate_target_satisfaction = small_permanent_target_satisfaction
		global_pop_food_consumption = -0.05
		pop_join_rebel_threshold = 0.05
	}

	# The seasonal handoff disrupted by an ambitious cacique refusing to
	# yield at the equinox, exogamy weakening as faction-marriages cluster
	# kin within one moiety, the rival kivas no longer recognising each
	# other's calendars -- the village's structural balance fails and any
	# central authority trying to govern through it finds two pueblos
	# where there should be one.
	negative_modifier = {
		scale = {
			value = 1
			subtract = scope:maintenance
		}
		crown_estate_satisfaction_decay = 0.01
		global_separatism = 0.1
	}
}

# 4. Knotted Cord Runner Network -- the cross-pueblo coordination institution
#    descended from Po'pay's organization of the 1680 Pueblo Revolt. From
#    his refuge at Taos the Tewa religious leader Po'pay sent runners
#    south and west bearing knotted yucca-fibre cords with a knot for each
#    day remaining until the rising; pueblos accepted the cord by
#    untying knots in turn, and on the agreed day -- August 10, 1680 --
#    Tewa, Tiwa, Towa, Keresan, Zuni, and Hopi pueblos rose together
#    against the Spanish from Taos to the Hopi mesas, three hundred miles
#    of desert coordinated by a knotted string. PUE preserves the runner
#    network as a standing institution: a corps of swift-runners trained
#    from boyhood in every pueblo, knotted cords as the medium of
#    inter-pueblo summons, and a coordinating council at the Taos
#    pueblo that the Spaniards never reached.

# 5. Pecos Plains Trade Intermediary -- the eastern-frontier institution
#    by which the easternmost pueblos (Pecos in particular, with Cuartelejo
#    and Picuris) brokered exchange between the agricultural pueblo world
#    and the bison-hunting peoples of the southern plains. At the autumn
#    trade fairs, held at Pecos in October and Taos in summer, Apache de
#    Cuartelejo, Apache Vaquero, Jicarilla, and from c. 1700 Comanche and
#    Wichita brought bison hides, dried meat, tallow, captives, and slaves
#    in exchange for maize, beans, squash, woven cotton mantas, ground
#    pigment, and the turquoise mined at Cerrillos. The fairs were
#    multi-week affairs at which a temporary trade-language emerged; the
#    pueblos collected tolls and a percentage of the through-traffic; and
#    Pecos in particular grew wealthy as the gateway between two worlds.
#    Spanish governors repeatedly tried to suppress the fairs as occasions
#    of Apache slave-trading and pueblo backsliding, and as repeatedly
#    failed.
