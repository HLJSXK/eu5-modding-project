# National Destinies -- Oyo Empire bureaucracies
# Six historical institutions of the Yoruba imperium of Old Oyo (c.1400-1836):
# the constitutional Oyo Mesi council, the Ogboni cult-judiciary, the Eso /
# Aare-Ona-Kakanfo military duumvirate, the Ilari title-bearer agents of the
# Alaafin, the Ajele provincial residency over vassal kingdoms, and the
# Babalawo Ifa divinatory priesthood. The auto-modifier grants 2 slots; Oyo-Ile
# contributes 1 = 3 effective. The player picks 3 of 6. The set models a state
# where royal authority (Alaafin) was permanently checked by hereditary
# council, secret society, and warrior offices situated outside the capital --
# a balance of estates that no other West African polity replicated in the
# same shape.

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Oyo Mesi -- the constitutional council of seven hereditary chiefs
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# The Oyo Mesi were the seven non-royal lineage-elders of metropolitan Oyo --
# the Bashorun (prime minister and de facto head), the Agbaakin, the Samu, the
# Alapini, the Laguna, the Akiniku, and the Asipa -- whose offices were
# hereditary within seven specific clans of the capital. They constituted the
# Alaafin's standing privy council, ratified his accession, controlled the
# state treasury, and most famously possessed the constitutional right to
# present the king with a calabash of parrot's eggs (the aki) -- a coded
# command for him to commit suicide. The Bashorun Gaha exercised this
# prerogative serially from c.1754 to 1774 before Alaafin Abiodun overthrew
# him. The Mesi's institutional permanence, more than the Alaafin's person,
# made Oyo's central government legible to its provinces.

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Ogboni Society -- the cult-judicial brotherhood of the earth
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# The Ogboni (also Osugbo among the Egba and Ijebu) was a secret initiatory
# society of senior men sworn to the earth-deity Onile. In Old Oyo and across
# the Yoruba towns the lodge -- iledi -- functioned as a court of last
# instance for capital cases, a counterweight to the Alaafin, and the body
# that pronounced the metaphysical verdict required before any king could be
# deposed. Membership was advertised by the edan Ogboni, paired bronze
# figurines worn around the neck. The society's cult of the earth made oaths
# sworn before it inviolable and gave its rulings a sanction the throne's
# decrees could not override; in the Egba refoundation at Abeokuta after
# 1830, Ogboni magistrates effectively ran the city's government.

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Eso and Aare-Ona-Kakanfo -- the cavalry corps and the field-general office
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# The Alaafin's military arm was the Eso, a corps of seventy titled cavalry
# officers personally appointed by the king and under no obligation to the
# Oyo Mesi. Above them stood the Aare-Ona-Kakanfo, the supreme field-general
# whose constitutional duty was to win battles or commit suicide. Crucially,
# the Kakanfo was forbidden to quarter inside the capital -- by custom he
# commanded from a provincial seat (Iwoye, Ikoyi, later Ilorin). This
# permanent geographical separation between throne and field-army was meant
# to prevent military coups against the Alaafin; in practice it eventually
# produced the opposite result when Aare Afonja revolted from Ilorin in 1817,
# starting the chain of disasters that ended Old Oyo. The Eso supplied Oyo's
# cavalry advantage from the Nupe horse-trade across the savanna.

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Ilari -- the dyed-head title-bearers of the Alaafin
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# The Ilari were several hundred royal officials -- some male, some female --
# whose distinguishing mark was a head shaved on one side and the other half
# dyed with indigo or camwood. They were drawn from royal slaves, captives,
# and clients (never from the metropolitan free clans of the Mesi), and they
# served as the Alaafin's eyes, mouth, and fist in the provinces: messengers,
# tribute-collectors, resident ambassadors, and executors of royal warrants.
# An Ilari entering a vassal-town with the king's staff held the Alaafin's
# direct authority; when posted as Ajele over a tributary kingdom, an Ilari
# became the throne's permanent local viceroy. Because their offices were
# personal and revocable rather than hereditary, the Ilari corps was the
# Alaafin's bureaucratic counterweight to the Mesi's lineage-based power.

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Ajele -- provincial residents over the tributary kingdoms
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# The Ajele were senior Ilari posted as permanent residents to the courts of
# the empire's tributary kings -- the kings of Egba, Egbado, Onko, Ijebu,
# Sabe, Ketu, Owu, and most importantly the Fon kingdom of Dahomey, which
# paid annual tribute to Oyo from c.1730 to 1818. The Ajele's office combined
# ambassador, supervisor, and tax-farmer: he assessed the annual tribute,
# audited the local king's accounts, attended court ceremonies as the
# Alaafin's representative, and forwarded annual remittances of slaves,
# cowries, cloth, and ivory to Oyo-Ile. The system gave Old Oyo the longest
# imperial reach of any Yoruba state -- from the Niger to the coast -- but
# the resentment Ajele extortion stirred in tributary capitals was a major

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Babalawo Ifa Council -- the divinatory priesthood of the Sixteen Odu
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# The Babalawo ("father of secrets") were the priests of Ifa, the Yoruba
# divinatory cult that read the cast of sixteen palm-nuts (or the opele
# divining-chain) against a corpus of 256 Odu, each Odu carrying its own
# verses, prescriptions, and precedents. At Oyo-Ile a recognized college of
# senior babalawo -- the Awo -- consulted Ifa on every major royal decision:
# the choice of a new Alaafin from among the eligible princes, the timing of
# wars, the propitiation needed to lift a famine, the signs against a
# tributary's loyalty. Ifa's authority crossed every Yoruba kingdom and was
# respected even by the Fon (who called it Fa) and the Edo. The Awo's annual
# consultation at Ile-Ife (the cult's spiritual capital) bound the empire's
# religious life to a single divinatory tradition older than any of its
# political institutions.
