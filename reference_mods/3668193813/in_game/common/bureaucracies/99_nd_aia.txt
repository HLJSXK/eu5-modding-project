# 1. Sharifate of Mecca and Custody of the Two Holy Mosques
#
# Founded in 1201 when Sharif Qatadah ibn Idris seized Mecca from the
# Banu Thaqif, the Hashemite Sharifate ruled the Hejaz continuously
# until 1925. The Sharif drew his religious legitimacy from descent
# of the Prophet through al-Hasan ibn Ali, and exercised practical
# custody (sidana) of the Kaaba and the Prophet's Mosque. Under
# Mamluk and later Ottoman suzerainty (the Sultan styled himself
# Khadim al-Haramayn al-Sharifayn after 1517), the Sharif
# administered Hajj logistics, distributed the surrah of gold and
# alms forwarded by Damascus and Cairo, and adjudicated among the
# resident ulema and the great waqf endowments of the Two Sanctuaries.
# =====================================================================

#
# In September 1597, al-Mansur al-Qasim, a Zaydi Sayyid of the Rassid
# line, proclaimed the Imamate at Sa'da in protest against the Ottoman

# Qasimid imams had recovered the highlands and signed a ten-year
# truce with the Porte; by 1636 they had expelled Ottoman garrisons

# Sayyid scholar-judges who claimed Hashemite descent and dispensed
# Zaydi-Hadawi jurisprudence from district centres at Sa'da, Sanaa,

# =====================================================================
# 3. Ibadhi Imamate of Nizwa
#
# The Ibadhi Imamate of Oman, first proclaimed in 750 CE and revived
# repeatedly from 793, was an elective theocracy headed by an Imam
# chosen by the assembly of ulema (the ahl al-hall wa-l-aqd) and
# confirmable or removable by the same body. The capital alternated
# between Nizwa and Rustaq; provincial authority was devolved to
# walis appointed by the Imam from among the scholarly families. A
# rotating circle of qadis applied Ibadhi jurisprudence from texts
# composed at Nizwa's school. The Imamate held that absolute
# political and military power should never rest with any one man,
# producing a markedly decentralised government persisting alongside
# the coastal Sultanate of Muscat after 1744.

# 4. Bedouin Dirah and Hima Pasture-Rights System
#
# The dirah was the customary tribal territory of the Najdi and
# Bedouin confederacies -- not a fixed border but an elastic claim
# over grazing grounds, watering points, and seasonal pastures
# enforced by tribal raiding (ghazu) and shaykhly arbitration. The
# hima ("protected ground") set aside reserves used only in named
# seasons for named animals. Wells were marked, blood-debts adjudicated
# in the majlis, and access negotiated between shaykhs at neutral
# fairs. No central state could function in the Najd without binding
# the great confederacies -- Anaiza, Shammar, Mutair, Qahtan -- by
# subsidy or arms; settled regimes that ignored the dirah lost their
# revenue from the desert margins within a generation.
# =====================================================================

# 5. Mocha Customs Administration
#
# After the Ottoman conquest of Yemen in 1538, every Red Sea ship
# was required by decree to put in at Mocha (al-Mukha) and pay
# customs there. Through the seventeenth century, Mocha grew into
# the world's coffee port: roughly 10,000 tonnes of beans annually
# moved through its warehouses, parched or roasted before export to
# prevent germination outside Yemen. After 1636 the Qasimid imams
# inherited the office of nakhuda al-bunduq (the customs commandant)
# and continued the Ottoman regime: a divan of three officials --
# the wali, the muhtasib, and the nakhuda al-bahr -- assessing duties
# at five to ten per cent on imports and a flat tariff per bahar of
# coffee. European factories at Mocha (English, Dutch, French) paid
# the same fee structure into the eighteenth century.
# =====================================================================

# 6. Diriyah Pact (Wahhabi-Saudi Compact)
#
# In 1744 (1157 AH), at the oasis town of Ad-Diriyah in Najd,
# Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab and the local emir Muhammad ibn Saud

# the dawah, the emir would provide arms and political headship. The
# compact created the First Saudi State (1744-1818) and a fused
# religio-political apparatus in which mutawwiun (enforcers of
# orthodoxy) rode with Saudi commanders, qadis appointed by the
# House of al-Sheikh applied a strict Hanbali jurisprudence, and
# revenue was extracted as zakat from the conquered tribes. The
# Ikhwan brotherhoods that grew from this compact carried Wahhabi
# doctrine across the peninsula by the early nineteenth century.
