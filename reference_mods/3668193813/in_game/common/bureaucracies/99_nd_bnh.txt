# National Destinies -- Beth Nahrain (Syriac Christian Mesopotamia) bureaucracies
#
# Tier 1 of the Syriac restoration chain. Four institutions grounded in
# real medieval Syriac Christian practice. Auto-modifier grants 2 slots;
# capital adds 1 = 3 effective. Player picks 3 of 4.
#
#   1. bnh_catholicate_of_the_east  -- Church of the East patriarchate (Mosul)
#   2. bnh_maphrianate_of_tagrit    -- Syriac Orthodox Maphrian primacy
#   3. bnh_tur_abdin_defenders      -- Mor Gabriel monastic-militia network
#   4. bnh_aramaic_chancery         -- Syriac scriptorial chancery
#
# All IDs prefixed bnh_ to avoid collision with vanilla and other mod content.

# =====================================================================
# 1. Catholicate of the East -- the Patriarchate of Seleucia / Mosul
#
# The Catholicos-Patriarch of the East was the universal head of the
# Church of the East, originally enthroned at Seleucia-Ctesiphon and
# later transferred to Baghdad and finally Mosul (Rabban Hormizd
# monastery). At its medieval height the Catholicate sent metropolitan
# bishops as far as Samarkand, Khanbaliq (Beijing) and Kerala. The
# patriarch acted as both spiritual head and millet-style civil judge
# for Christians under Muslim rule. Refounding the Catholicate as a
# state organ centralizes ecclesiastical authority in the crown's seat
# and formalizes the church's parallel administration of marriage,
# inheritance and Christian-on-Christian litigation. The clergy gain
# enormous structural power; rural notables resent the diversion of
# tithe and judicial fees to the patriarchal court.
# =====================================================================

	implementation_price = price:implement_bureaucracy_price

		value = country_economical_base

# =====================================================================
# 2. Maphrianate of Tagrit -- the Syriac Orthodox primacy of the East
#
# The Maphrian of Tagrit (later moved to Mosul and Mor Mattai monastery)
# was the ranking Syriac Orthodox prelate east of the Euphrates, second
# only to the Patriarch of Antioch and effectively autonomous in
# Mesopotamian affairs. Bar Hebraeus (Gregorios Abu al-Faraj) held the
# office in the 13th century and used it to write the Chronography and
# Ecclesiastical History from Mor Mattai. The Maphrianate functioned as
# the Mesopotamian-Syriac counterpart to the Antiochene patriarchate,
# defending the eastern dioceses' rite and language against Greek and
# Latin influence. Refounded as a royal bureau, it raises learned
# clerics, codifies Syriac canon law, and shields the eastern church
# from foreign-patriarchal interference -- but the resulting friction
# with the see of Antioch reduces the kingdom's standing among other
# Christian powers.

# =====================================================================
# 3. Tur Abdin Monastic Defenders -- the Mor Gabriel mountain network
#
# The Tur Abdin ("Mountain of the Servants of God") plateau between
# Nusaybin and the Tigris held the densest concentration of Syriac
# Orthodox monasteries in the medieval world: Mor Gabriel (founded
# 397), Deir al-Zafaran (Mor Hananyo), Mor Yaqub of Salah, and dozens
# of village churches. Through the Artuqid, Ilkhan, Aq Qoyunlu and
# Ottoman periods the Tur Abdin functioned as a self-policing
# Christian highland: monastic abbots adjudicated disputes, village
# militias defended the cliff-monasteries, and the bishop of Tur
# Abdin functioned as a de facto regional governor. Royal recognition
# transforms the network into a permanent frontier institution -- a
# defensive belt that the crown does not directly garrison. The
# bishop-abbots gain substantial autonomy at the expense of central
# revenue extraction.

# =====================================================================
# 4. Aramaic-Syriac Chancery -- the scriptorial administration
#
# Syriac was the working administrative and liturgical language of
# Christian Mesopotamia from late antiquity through the Mongol period.
# The chancery tradition of the Church of the East and the Syriac
# Orthodox scriptoria (Mor Mattai, Mor Gabriel, Deir al-Zafaran) trained
# scribes who served not only the church but also the Sasanian, Abbasid
# and Ilkhanid courts. Bar Hebraeus's "Cream of Wisdom" and the
# bilingual Syriac-Arabic court correspondence of the 13th century
# document a literate clerical-bureaucratic class that translated Greek
# philosophy, Persian administrative practice and Arabic statecraft
# into the working language of the Mesopotamian church. A revived
# chancery institutionalizes this scribal class as a permanent civil
# service drawn from the urban Christian burgher and clerical
# households of Mosul, Edessa and Nusaybin. When neglected, the
# scriptoria slide into copying-for-fees and cabinet output suffers.
